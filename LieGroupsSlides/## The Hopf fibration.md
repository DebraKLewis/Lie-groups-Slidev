## The Hopf fibration

The homotopy group $\pi_3(S^2) \simeq {\mathbb Z} \, {}$ is generated by a simple map with rich structure.

Many different characterizations of the fibration exist, revealing different aspects of its structure.

We'll use the Hopf fibration as a motivating example for fiber bundles, particularly principal bundles, briefly noting the relationship between certain cohomology groups, called characteristic classes, and the structure of principal bundles.
<br>

The *Hopf fibration* $\ p\, : S^3 \to S^2 \, {}$ can be naively implemented as
$$
p(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}) := \left (2 (x_1 \, x_3 + x_2 \, x_4 ), 2(x_2 x_3 - x_1 x_4),  x_1^2 + x_2^2 - x_3^2 - x_4^2 \right ).
$$

Up to reordering of terms, this is supposedly the form in which Hopf originally expressed the map, but it's not particularly revealing. 

---

If we makes the identifications
$$
\begin{align}
S^3 &\simeq \{ (z_0, z_1) \in {\mathbb C}^2: |z_0|^2 + |z_1|^2 = 1 \} \\
&(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})  \mapsto (x_{1}+ix_{2},x_{3}+ix_{4})
\end{align}
$$
and
$$
\begin{align}
S^2 &\simeq \{ (z, x) \in {\mathbb C}×{\mathbb R} :  |z|^2 + x^2 = 1 \}\\
&(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\mapsto (x_{1}+ix_{2},x_{3}),
\end{align}
$$

then the Hopf fibration takes the form
$$
p(z_{0},z_{1})=(2z_{0}z_{1}^{\ast },\left|z_{0}\right|^{2}-\left|z_{1}\right|^{2}).
$$

Hence
$$
p(z_0, z_1) = p(w_0, w_1) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad (w_0, w_1) =(λ z_0, λ z_ 1) \ \mbox{ for some }  λ \in S^1 \subset {\mathbb C}.
$$
<br>

Preimages $\, p^{−1}(z, x) \simeq S^1\,{}$ are neither discrete groups, as for covering spaces, nor vector spaces, as for vector bundles. They're all circles.

## Fiber bundles

Given be a connected space  $B$ with basepoint $b_0$, a surjective, continuous map $p : E → B$ is a *locally trivial fibration*, or *fiber bundle*, with *fiber* $\,F := p^{−1}(b_0)\,{}$  
if for every $x ∈ B$ there is an open neighborhood $U_x ⊂ B$ and a homeomorphism
$$
Ψ_{U_x} : p^{−1}(U_x) → U_x × F
$$
such that the following diagram commutes:
![h:220](Images/whiteSpace.png)![h:220](Images/whiteSpace.png)![h:220](Images/fiberBundleCommDiag.png)

$B$ is called the *base space* and $E$ is the *total space*. 

---

A map (or “morphism”) of fiber bundles $\, Φ : (F_1, E_1, B_1) → (F_2, E_2, B_2\,{}$) 
is a pair of basepoint-preserving continuous maps 
$$
φ : B_1 → B_2  \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad \tilde φ : E_1 → E_2
$$
satisfying
$$
\begin{align}
E_1& \xrightarrow{\ \tilde φ\ } E_2 \\
p_1\downarrow\ & \qquad \downarrow p_2 \\ 
B_1& \xrightarrow{\ φ\ } B_2
\end{align} .
$$
<br>
A (local) *section* $\,s : U \subseteq B → E \, {}$ of a fiber bundle $\, p : E → B\,{}$ is a continuous map satisfying
$$
p ◦ s = \mbox{id}_B|_U.
$$

A fiber bundle $(F, E, B)$ is *trivial* if it isomorphic to the fibration $\, B×F → B$. 

---

### Examples of fiber bundles

- Vector bundles, with $F$ a vector space. Vector fields are sections of vector bundles.
- Covering spaces, with $F$ the group of deck transformations.  
Lifts are sections of covering spaces. 
- The M&ouml;bius band, with $B = S^1$ and $F = [-1, 1]$.
- Complex projective spaces are the base spaces of fiber bundles.   
$\, {\mathbb C} P^n \,{}$ is the orbit space of the diagonal action of $\, F = {\mathbb C} - \{0 \}\,{}$ by multiplication on $\, E = {\mathbb C}^{n+1} - \{ 0 \}$. Local section:
![h:150](Images/whiteSpace.png)![h:150](Images/whiteSpace.png)![h:150](Images/CPnSection.png)
- $B = {\mathbb C} P^n, \,{} E = S^{2n + 1}, \ {}$ and $F = S^1 \simeq \{ z \in {\mathbb C} : |z| = 1 \}$. 
Since ${\mathbb C} P^1 \simeq S^2$, for $n = 1$ this is the Hopf fibration.

## Principal bundles

A *principal $G$-bundle* is a fiber bundle $p : E → B$ with fiber $G$ satisfying

- $G$ is a topological group acting freely on $E,$ with 
$$G \cdot z = p^{-1}(p(z)) \qquad \qquad \forall \ z \in E,$$ 
&nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp;&nbsp; and free and transitive induced actions 
$$µ_x : p^{−1}(x) × G → p^{−1}(x) \qquad \qquad \forall \ x \in B$$ 
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; on the fibers.
- There are equivariant local trivializations $\, ψ : p^{−1}(U) \xrightarrow{\simeq} U \times G, \, {}$ i.e.
$$
\begin{align}
p^{−1}(U) \times G & \xrightarrow{\ ψ \times \rm{id}_G\ } U \times G \times G \\
µ \downarrow\ & \qquad \downarrow {\scriptsize \rm{id}_U \times \rm{mult}} \\ 
p^{−1}(U)& \xrightarrow{\quad ψ\quad } U \times G
\end{align} 
$$

---

**Very important example**: &nbsp; If $E$ is a smooth manifold and $G$ is a compact Lie group acting freely and smoothly on $E$, then the projection $\, p : E → E/G\,{}$ is a principal $G$-bundle.
<br>

**A special case**: &nbsp; If $H$ is a compact subgroup of a Lie group $G$, then $\, p : G → G/H\,{}$ 
is a principal $H$-bundle.
<br>

***The Hopf fibration as a quotient of Lie groups*** 

The Hopf fibration can be obtained by considering rotations of $S^2$ in $\, {\mathbb R}^3$. 

Specifically, the Hopf fibration $p\, : S^3 \to S^2 \, {}$ is a principal $SO(2)$-bundle, where we identify $S^1$ with rotations about a given axis.

This avatar of the Hopf fibration is relevant to both classical and quantum mechanics, 
and is a very special case of the previous example.

---

- The spin group is used in quantum mechanics to describe the symmetries of (electrically neutral, uncharged) fermions. 
- $\mbox{Spin}(n)\,{}$ is a double cover of $SO(n)$. 
- For $n > 2$, $\mbox{Spin}(n)\,{}$ is simply connected, and thus is the universal cover of $SO(n)$.
<br>

$\mbox{Spin}(3)\,{}$ is diffeomorphic to $S^3$, and can be identified with the group $\mbox{Sp}(1)\,{}$ of unit quaternions, or with the special unitary group $\, SU(2). \,{}$ We'll use unit quaternions.

If we identify ${\mathbb R}^4$ with ${\mathbb H}$ by
$$
{\mathbf x} \mapsto q=x_{1}+x_{2}\mathbf {i} +x_{3}\mathbf {j} + x_{4}\mathbf {k},
$$
$S^3$ corresponds to the group $\, \mbox{Sp}(1)\,{}$ of *versors*, the unit quaternions.

Given a versor $q$, define $\phi_q: {\mathbb R}^3 \to {\mathbb R}^3$ by
$$
{\mathbf x} \mapsto \psi^{-1}(q \, \psi({\mathbf x}) \, q^*), \qquad \mbox{where} \qquad 
\psi(x_1, x_2, x_3) :=  x_1 \, \mathbf {i} + x_2 \, \mathbf {j} + x_3 \, \mathbf {k}. 
$$

---

Since 
$$
|q p q^∗|^2 = q p q^∗ q p^∗ q^∗ = q p p^∗ q^∗ = |p|^2
$$
for any versor $q$ and $p \in {\mathbb H}, \ \phi_q\,{}$ is an isometry. In fact, $\phi_q$ is a rotation.
<br>

A choice of ${\mathbf x} \in S^2$ determines a map 
$$
\begin{align}
\Phi_{\mathbf x}& : S^3 \to S^2\\
q & \mapsto \phi_q({\mathbf x}).
\end{align}
$$

A versor $p$ is fixed by the subgroup
$$
\begin{align}
\mbox{Spin}(3)_p &= \{q  \in S^3 \simeq \mbox{Spin}(3) : q p q^*  = p \} \\
&= \{ \cos \theta + \sin \theta \, p : \theta \in S^1 \} \simeq SO(2).
\end{align}
$$

Thus the Hopf fibration can be interpreted as the principal $SO(2)$-bundle.
$$
\Phi_{\mathbf x}: G \to G/G_{\psi({\mathbf x})} \qquad \qquad \mbox{for} \qquad G = \mbox{Spin}(3).
$$

---


A principal bundle is trivial $\ \ \Longleftrightarrow \ \ {}$ it has a global section.  

If $G$ acts on the left, the isomorphism determined by the section $\, s: B \to E\ {}$ is
$$
z \mapsto (p(z), g) \qquad \mbox{for $\ g\ {}$ satisfying} \ \ g^{-1} \cdot z = s(p(z)).
$$
<br>

A *characteristic class* is a cohomology class of a principal bundle $E$ that measures the deviation of the local product structure from a global product structure, i.e. obstructions to the existence of a global section. 
<br>

The Hopf fibration can be shown to be a generator of $\pi_3(S^2)$ by showing that a particular characteristic class, the Hopf invariant, takes the value 1 on the Hopf fibration.
