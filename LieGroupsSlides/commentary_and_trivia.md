## The shape of a Lie group - looking ahead to representation theory

We're seeing the Lie bracket of the Lie algebra turning up in a lot of contexts, and we're going to see a lot more of that in the next few lectures. The relationship between the Lie bracket of fg and the noncommutivity of G is more or less direct, but there's other fundamental relationships between the bracket and local curvature (in various mathematical senses of the word) of G. 

The most visual/tactile interpretation of curvature involves local approximation of a surface by "canonical surfaces". We're all familiar with the tangent plane at a point on a surface as the best linear approximation to the surface at that point. The curvature of the surface at a point is the reciprocal of the radius of the best spherical approximation to the surface at that point. If the surface bends a lot, the curvature will be large; if the surface is almost flat, the curvature will be close to zero. 

Parallel transport of a vector along a closed curve on a sphere typically yields a different vector after following the loop than the vector we started with. That mismatch is relevant to many area of mathematics and physics. The Lie bracket determines the analogous mismatch after parallel transport along loops in Lie groups. 

Representation theory can, pragmatically, be regarded as a collection of efficient techiques and results for extraction and organization of information about Lie algebras. Representation theory is beautiful and important in its own right, but the importance of the Lie algebra structure in so many contexts and applications makes efficient analysis of that structure extremely valuable. Representation theory and some of the geometric results we're currently developing will allow us to ignore a lot of detail, because the algebra structure calls so many of the shots.

Angular momentum, gyroscopes and guidance systems

Most A-4/V2 rocket/missile launches relied exclusively on an internal guidance system, rather than a guiding beam, remote real-time steering, etc. Advantage: Opponents can't knock out the steering by disrupting the guiding beam, shutting off your Starshield service, and so on. The A-4/V2 used a multi-gyroscope system to determine the velocity and orientation of the rocket and correct course as needed by moving the tail fins; this was all analog! Conservation of angular momentum kept the gyros in more or less fixed orientations, and various electrical goodies measured the movement of the rocket around the gyros. (This 15 minute video is well worth watching.)

The gyros and steering mechanisms became more sophisticated during the Cold War, but I think the same general approach was employed. Small, cheap MEMS gyroscopes (https://www.skymems.com/what-is-a-mems-gyroscope/) are now used in all sorts of vehicles and devices, including smartphones.

Personal historical trivia/perspective: Our house for most of my childhood was a 5.7 mile drive (according to Google Maps) from the Oat Mountain Nike Missile Site LA-88 (https://www.themilitarystandard.com/missile/nike/sites/la-88-l/) "The Department of Defense narrative identifies LA-88 at Chatsworth as the first permanent operational Nike battery in the nation." It entered service in 1957 and was removed from service in 1974. Oat Mountain intially hosted Ajax missiles with conventional warheads, intended to defend against strategic bombers; it later converted to Hercules missiles carrying 20 megaton nuclear warheads, for use against bombers or tactical ballistic missles. Ajax and Hercules were steered from the ground, which involved coordinated tracking of multiple very rapidly moving objects, but still relied on gyros for "proprioception". A successful Hercules-incoming ballistic missile interception would have gone roughly like the climatic scene in The Iron Giant (, from 2:00). The Great Shake Out drill yesterday reminded me of the "drop drills" when I was growing up; hence this line of thought. "Duck and cover" - facing away from the windows - presumably wouldn't have done much good if a bomber or BM got through, but might have helped if there were detonation on interception. The drive up Brown's Canyon to the gates of the Oat Mountain complex was sometimes part of our Sunday family drives - we'd all pile into my dad's Corvair convertible after our parents had had a few drinks, and zip around the local canyon roads. (The Corvair was the subject of Ralph Nader's Unsafe at Any Speed, in large part due to its tendency to roll if the tires weren't properly inflated; the convertible had neither seat belts nor a roll bar.) The Valley was un-Spartan in many regards, but there was a certain "η τάν η επί τας", "with it [your shield] or on it" vibe.
